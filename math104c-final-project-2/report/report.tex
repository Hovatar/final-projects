\section{Introduction}
In this project I compare finite-difference methods for two model PDEs: the
Laplace equation (elliptic) and the heat equation (parabolic). For the Laplace
problem I use the five-point stencil and solve the resulting linear system with
Gauss--Seidel iteration. For the heat equation I implement the explicit
Forward-Difference method, the implicit Backward-Difference method, and the
Crank--Nicolson method, and I solve the tridiagonal systems with the Crout
factorization (Algorithm~6.7). My goals are to compare accuracy across methods,
to check the stability of the Forward-Difference method against the theoretical
condition $\lambda \le \tfrac12$, to look at how implicit methods behave for
larger time steps, and to confirm the $O(h^2)$ spatial accuracy of the
five-point stencil on a problem where it is actually visible.

All methods were coded in Python; the code is described in
Section~\ref{sec:code}. The sanity-check script reproduces the closed-form
solution $u(x,y) = 100\,x\,y$ of Problem~A and shows $O(h^2)$ convergence on
the Poisson manufactured-solution check in Problem~C2.

\section{The Methods}\label{sec:methods}
Throughout I write $w_{ij} \approx u(x_i, y_j)$ for the elliptic problem and
$w_{i,j} \approx u(x_i, t_j)$ for the heat equation. The spatial step is $h$
and (where applicable) the time step is $k$. For the heat equation,
\[
\lambda \;=\; \frac{k}{h^{2}}.
\]

\paragraph{Five-point stencil (Laplace, $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$).}
Replacing each second derivative by the standard centered second difference
and multiplying through by $h^2$ gives the discrete equation at every interior
node,
\[
4\,w_{ij} \;-\; w_{i+1,j} \;-\; w_{i-1,j} \;-\; w_{i,j+1} \;-\; w_{i,j-1}
\;=\; 0,
\]
which we solve by Gauss--Seidel iteration with the relaxation
\[
w_{ij}^{\text{new}} \;=\; \tfrac14\bigl(
w_{i+1,j} + w_{i-1,j} + w_{i,j+1} + w_{i,j-1}\bigr),
\]
sweeping over the interior in lexicographic order and stopping when the
sup-norm change between sweeps drops below $10^{-8}$. I also include an SOR
variant with relaxation $\omega = 2/(1+\sin(\pi h))$ for comparison. The local
truncation error of the five-point stencil is $-\tfrac{h^{2}}{12}
(u_{xxxx}+u_{yyyy}) + O(h^{4})$, i.e.\ $O(h^{2})$ in general.

\paragraph{Forward-Difference (heat equation).}
A forward time difference and a centered space difference give the explicit
update
\[
w_{i,j+1} \;=\; (1 - 2\lambda)\,w_{i,j}
\;+\; \lambda\bigl(w_{i+1,j} + w_{i-1,j}\bigr),
\]
with $\lambda = k/h^{2}$. Each new value is an explicit weighted average of
neighbouring old values, so no linear system is solved at each step. The local
truncation error is $O(k + h^{2})$, and the von Neumann stability analysis
gives the conditional stability bound $\lambda \le \tfrac12$.

\paragraph{Backward-Difference (heat equation).}
Using a backward time difference instead gives the implicit update
\[
-\lambda\,w_{i-1,j+1} \;+\; (1 + 2\lambda)\,w_{i,j+1}
\;-\; \lambda\,w_{i+1,j+1}
\;=\; w_{i,j},
\]
for $i = 1,\dots,m-1$. This is a tridiagonal system with diagonal
$1+2\lambda$ and off-diagonals $-\lambda$, which I solve by the Crout
factorization (Algorithm~6.7) at every time step. The local truncation error
is $O(k + h^{2})$, but the scheme is unconditionally stable.

\paragraph{Crank--Nicolson (heat equation).}
Averaging the explicit and implicit schemes (Crank--Nicolson is the trapezoid
rule in time applied to the semi-discrete ODE) gives
\[
-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,w_{i-1,j+1} \;+\; (1+\lambda)\,w_{i,j+1}
\;-\; \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,w_{i+1,j+1}
\;=\;
\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,w_{i-1,j} \;+\; (1-\lambda)\,w_{i,j}
\;+\; \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,w_{i+1,j}.
\]
Again the left-hand side is tridiagonal and is solved by Crout. The local
truncation error is $O(k^{2} + h^{2})$, so the scheme is one full order more
accurate in time than the Backward-Difference method, and it is also
unconditionally stable.

\section{Problem A: Laplace on the Unit Square}
Consider
\[
u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0, \qquad 0 < x < 1, \quad 0 < y < 1,
\]
with $u(0,y) = u(x,0) = 0$, $u(x,1) = 100\,x$, and $u(1,y) = 100\,y$. A quick
observation makes this problem unusually convenient: the function $u(x,y) =
100\,x\,y$ is harmonic ($u_{xx}=u_{yy}=0$) and matches all four boundary
conditions, including the corner $(1,1)$ where the two non-zero edges agree on
the value $100$. So this is the exact analytic solution. I use it as the
reference for the error tables.

$8 \times 10^{-9}$, which is essentially the Gauss--Seidel stopping tolerance.

Two things are visible in Table~\ref{tab:Aref}. First, the error does not
shrink like $h^{2}$ as $h$ is halved; in fact it grows slightly. That is not a
contradiction of the $O(h^{2})$ local truncation error analysis: the
five-point stencil is exact for any polynomial of degree at most three, and
$100\,x\,y$ is bilinear, so the discrete equation is satisfied exactly by the
true solution. What the table is measuring is therefore the iteration error
from Gauss--Seidel, not the discretization error, and the iteration error
grows mildly with the problem size at fixed tolerance. To see genuine
$O(h^{2})$ convergence of the same stencil I need a problem whose exact
solution is not polynomial; that is what Problem~C2 does.

Second, the iteration count grows quickly with refinement: $33$, $131$, $492$
at $h = 1/4, 1/8, 1/16$, roughly a factor of four per refinement. This is the
well-known $O(h^{-2})$ scaling of Gauss--Seidel on second-order elliptic
problems. Switching to SOR with the optimal $\omega = 2/(1+\sin(\pi h))$
dramatically reduces the count: $33 \to 17$ at $h = 1/4$, $131 \to 36$ at
$h = 1/8$, and $492 \to 70$ at $h = 1/16$. The benefit grows as the grid is
refined, which agrees with the theory that SOR converges in $O(h^{-1})$
iterations versus $O(h^{-2})$ for Gauss--Seidel.

\section{Problem B: Heat Equation}
Consider
\[
u_{t} = u_{xx}, \qquad 0 < x < 1, \quad t > 0,
\]
with $u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0$ and $u(x, 0) = \sin(\pi x)$. The exact solution
is $u(x,t) = e^{-\pi^{2} t} \sin(\pi x)$, a pure decay of the first Fourier
mode. I integrate to $T = 0.5$, by which time the exact peak value at
$x = 0.5$ has decayed from $1$ to $e^{-\pi^{2}/2} \approx 7.193\times 10^{-3}$.
All runs use $h = 0.1$ (so $m = 10$). For the time step I take $k = 0.0025$ in
the Forward stable case, giving $\lambda = 0.25 < \tfrac12$, and $k = 0.01$ in
every other case, giving $\lambda = 1$, which is well above the Forward
stability threshold but no obstacle for the implicit schemes.

all interior nodes, and Table~\ref{tab:Berr} summarises the max errors over
the whole space-time grid for all four runs. Figure~\ref{fig:B} shows the
final profiles, the blow-up of the unstable Forward run, and the
error-vs-time curves.

The Forward-Difference scheme behaves exactly as the stability theory
predicts. With $\lambda = 0.25$ the amplification factor of the $n$-th
Fourier mode is $1 - 4\lambda\sin^{2}(n\pi h/2)$, which lies in $(0, 1)$ for
every $n$, so all modes decay and the error stays around $1.5 \times 10^{-3}$
over the whole interval. With $\lambda = 1.0$ the highest representable mode
on this grid ($n = m - 1 = 9$) has amplification factor
$1 - 4\sin^{2}(9\pi/20) \approx -2.9$, very close to $-3$ in magnitude. The
worst-case factor of about $3$ per step amplifies any high-frequency
roundoff by roughly $3^{50}$ over the $50$ time steps, which is easily
enough to swamp the small true solution and produces the final
$|w|_{\infty} \approx 6 \times 10^{6}$.

The Backward-Difference and Crank--Nicolson methods both stay stable at
$\lambda = 1$, but their accuracies differ markedly: the max error is
$2.03 \times 10^{-2}$ for BD and $2.73 \times 10^{-3}$ for CN, almost an
order of magnitude smaller. This matches the theoretical truncation orders:
BD is $O(k + h^{2})$ and CN is $O(k^{2} + h^{2})$, so at $k = 0.01$ the
temporal error in BD is $\sim 10^{-2}$ while in CN it is $\sim 10^{-4}$,
leaving the spatial error $O(h^{2}) \sim 10^{-3}$ as the bottleneck for CN.
The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:B} shows that the error of the stable
Forward run is actually a bit better than BD over most of the interval.
That is not surprising, since the Forward run uses $k = 0.0025$, four times
smaller in time, but it costs four times as many time steps to get there.

\section{Problem C1: Multi-mode Initial Condition (Student-designed)}
For my first additional experiment I keep the heat equation but change the
initial condition to a sum of two Fourier modes,
\[
u(x, 0) \;=\; \sin(\pi x) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\sin(3\pi x),
\]
with exact solution
\[
u(x, t) \;=\; e^{-\pi^{2} t}\sin(\pi x) \;+\;
\tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-9\pi^{2} t}\sin(3\pi x).
\]
The point of this experiment is that the heat equation diagonalises in
the sine basis, so each Fourier mode decays at its own rate $e^{-n^{2}\pi^{2}t}$.
The $n = 3$ mode decays nine times faster than the $n = 1$ mode and, even
though it starts with half the amplitude, it is almost gone by $t = 0.05$.
This is interesting because (a) it gives a direct visual demonstration of why
implicit smoothers are so effective for elliptic problems, and (b) it lets me
check that Crank--Nicolson correctly damps high modes without the
overshooting that Forward-Difference would produce near the stability limit
on the high-frequency component.

I use Crank--Nicolson with $h = 0.05$, $k = 0.005$ (so $\lambda = 2.0$, well
outside the Forward stability region but harmless for CN), integrating to
$T = 0.2$. Table~\ref{tab:C1} shows the profile at $t = 0.05$ against the
exact solution.

\section{Problem C2: Poisson Equation with a Manufactured Solution
(Student-designed)}
For my second additional experiment I add a source term and solve the
Poisson equation,
\[
u_{xx} + u_{yy} \;=\; f(x, y) \quad\text{on } (0,1)^{2}, \qquad
u = 0 \text{ on the boundary},
\]
with $f$ chosen so that $u(x, y) = \sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)$ is the exact
solution. Plugging into the PDE gives
\[
f(x, y) \;=\; -2\pi^{2}\,\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
\]
This is a manufactured-solution experiment: I know the answer, so I can
isolate the discretisation error of the five-point stencil. Because the exact
solution is genuinely smooth and \emph{not} a low-degree polynomial, the
$O(h^{2})$ truncation error is no longer hidden by the special property that
saved Problem~A. The discrete equation becomes
\[
w_{ij} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(
w_{i+1,j}+w_{i-1,j}+w_{i,j+1}+w_{i,j-1} - h^{2} f_{ij}\bigr),
\]
which I again relax with Gauss--Seidel.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{Which methods are most accurate?} On the heat equation,
Crank--Nicolson was the most accurate per unit work: at the same $h$ and $k$
it produced an error roughly an order of magnitude smaller than
Backward-Difference. The Forward-Difference scheme can match CN when the
stability constraint is satisfied, but it costs a much smaller time step to
get there. On the elliptic side, the five-point stencil with Gauss--Seidel
gave essentially the exact answer for Problem~A and $O(h^{2})$ convergence
on the Poisson check.

\textbf{How does mesh refinement affect the error?} For the five-point
stencil applied to Poisson (Problem~C2), halving $h$ reduced the max error
by a factor of $4$, matching the theoretical $O(h^{2})$ rate. For the heat
equation, CN gave $O(k^{2} + h^{2})$ behaviour, so when $k$ and $h$ shrink
together by the same factor the error drops by roughly $4$. BD has only
$O(k + h^{2})$, so for the same shrink factor the temporal part of the error
only halves.

\textbf{Why does the Forward-Difference method become unstable when
$\lambda > \tfrac12$?} The amplification factor for the $n$-th Fourier mode
is $g_{n} = 1 - 4\lambda \sin^{2}(n\pi h/2)$. The most negative value occurs
at the highest mode and equals $1 - 4\lambda$; stability requires
$|g_{n}| \le 1$, i.e.\ $\lambda \le \tfrac12$. Beyond this, the high mode is
multiplied by a factor with $|g_{n}| > 1$ at every step, so any roundoff in
that mode is amplified exponentially. In my run this produced a final
$|w|_{\infty}$ of nearly $6 \times 10^{6}$ even though the exact peak at
$t = 0.5$ is below $10^{-2}$.

\textbf{Why do implicit methods stay stable for larger time steps?} For BD
the amplification factor is $g_{n} = 1/(1 + 4\lambda\sin^{2}(n\pi h/2))$,
which is in $(0, 1]$ for every $\lambda > 0$ and every $n$. For CN it is
$(1 - 2\lambda \sin^{2})/(1 + 2\lambda \sin^{2})$, which lies in $(-1, 1]$
for every $\lambda > 0$. So both are unconditionally stable, and the time
step is governed only by accuracy, not stability. That is what makes the
implicit schemes the natural choice when you need to take large time steps
or when the problem is stiff.

\textbf{How does stability differ between elliptic and parabolic PDEs?} The
elliptic problem has no time stepping, so stability instead shows up as the
question of whether the iterative solver converges and how fast.
Gauss--Seidel converges for the five-point stencil because the matrix is
strictly diagonally dominant (the row sum is $4$ on the diagonal and at most
$4$ off the diagonal). For the parabolic problem, stability is about whether
errors at one time step are amplified or damped by the marching operator,
which is exactly the Fourier analysis above.

\textbf{How do errors propagate over time?} For Forward-Difference with
$\lambda \le \tfrac12$ each mode is damped per step, so errors do not grow
(and the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:B} shows the max error actually
shrinks with time as the true solution itself decays). For BD and CN the
amplification factor is also bounded by $1$ in magnitude for every mode, so
again errors do not grow. When the Forward stability constraint is violated
the highest mode is amplified at every step, which is the standard
divergence in the middle panel of Figure~\ref{fig:B}.

\textbf{What tradeoffs exist?} Explicit methods are cheap per step but
require small $k$ for stability, so they may end up doing more total work.
Implicit methods cost a tridiagonal solve per step (linear in $m$ via Crout,
not $O(m^{3})$), but they let you pick $k$ for accuracy alone. CN buys an
extra order of temporal accuracy over BD at the same per-step cost, so it is
usually the right default for the heat equation unless something else (like
non-smooth initial data) makes its slightly more oscillatory damping
problematic.

\section{Conclusions}
The numerical experiments confirmed the theoretical pictures from class.
The five-point stencil is $O(h^{2})$ accurate on the Poisson check (Problem~C2)
and gave the exact answer on Problem~A because that problem's solution happens
to be bilinear. The Forward-Difference scheme is conditionally stable exactly
where $\lambda \le \tfrac12$, and crossing that threshold produces explosive
growth in the highest representable mode. Crank--Nicolson is unconditionally
stable and one order more accurate in time than Backward-Difference, which
shows up as a roughly $10\times$ smaller error at the same $h$ and $k$ in
Problem~B. The multi-mode experiment in Problem~C1 made the spectral structure
of the heat equation directly visible: the $n = 3$ mode decayed nine times
faster than the $n = 1$ mode, exactly as the analytic decay rates
$e^{-n^{2}\pi^{2}t}$ predict.
